Can a stock split halve an index even though nobody lost money? Work through a tiny basket, then design the system that keeps its history trustworthy. About 8 minutes.

1. One basket, two ways to weigh it

An index is a rules-based measurement, not necessarily a portfolio you can buy. A price-weighted index gives a higher-priced share more influence. A capitalization-weighted index gives more influence to a company with greater price × eligible shares. Prices and shares below are fictional, in one currency; shares are in millions.

Divisors stay fixed while you change prices or shares; changing shares here is a calculation experiment, not a modeled corporate action.

Check your prediction

At the defaults, A has ⅔ of the price weight, but B has ⅚ of the cap weight. A 10% rise in just A or just B moves each index in proportion to that starting weight. A simultaneous 10% rise in both moves both indexes by 10%.

2. A split is not a market return

A 2-for-1 split changes A from $100 × 1 million shares to $50 × 2 million shares. Its market value remains $100 million. The cap-weighted numerator stays at 600, so its divisor stays 6 and the index stays 100.

For the price-weighted index, the numerator falls from 150 to 100. Without adjustment its level would fall from 75 to 50. To preserve 75, set the new divisor to 100 ÷ 75 = 1⅓. Later price changes use that new divisor. Real corporate-action treatment depends on the methodology.

Recall: why adjust the divisor?

To preserve continuity across a non-market change—not to erase an actual market movement. Changes in constituents and some other corporate actions may also require an adjustment.

3. Turn the arithmetic into a reliable system

A calculation needs a defined snapshot: constituent set, prices, eligible shares, currency conversion, divisor, methodology version and effective time. Decide whether inputs are last trade, bid/mid or closing prices, and what stale or missing prices mean. Float factors, caps and rebalancing rules vary by index. A price-return index and a total-return index also treat dividends differently.

  1. Ingest: identify source and instrument, validate prices, track source sequence numbers, detect gaps and reconcile or replay according to the feed contract. Do not assume timestamps alone establish a global order.
  2. Calculate: apply an accepted update to the correct versioned state. For a fixed cap basket, update the numerator by (new price − old price) × eligible shares; recalculate from the full snapshot to reconcile. Specify decimal precision and rounding.
  3. Commit and publish: atomically persist the input position, state, result and an outbox record in one database transaction, if they share a database. Relay the outbox at least once; downstream consumers deduplicate by index and version. Database atomicity alone does not make external delivery exactly once.
  4. Recover: replay from durable inputs and versioned reference data. Define late ticks, corrections, corporate-action effective times and whether historical values are revised; keep an audit trail and fence competing leaders.
Design challenge: a tick arrives twice after a crash. What must remain true?

The same source event must not be applied twice, even if an outbox message is delivered twice. Persist the deduplication position with the state update; give outputs stable identities so consumers can deduplicate too. Gaps and corrections need their own explicit policy.

Take it with you

Price chooses influence; eligible market value chooses influence; the divisor preserves continuity; a versioned snapshot makes the result reproducible.
